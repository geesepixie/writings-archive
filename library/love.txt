Politics has always felt like walking through fog. You hear the voices — loud, certain, furious — yet the shapes behind them blur. Who’s right? Who’s wrong? You can’t quite tell. History, it seems, doesn’t deal in absolutes; it traffics in consequences.

Take Mohammed Ali. A man hailed as the founder of modern Egypt, yet shadowed by one of its darkest massacres. He slaughtered men of faith, the elderly, the defenseless — an act that should stain any legacy beyond repair. And yet, when you look at Egypt before him, it was little more than farmland under Ottoman chains. No schools, no industry, no notion of modern education. He changed that. He built, he organized, he dreamed of a nation that could stand on its own feet. The same hands that destroyed also shaped the foundation of what we now call Egypt.

It’s unsettling — the way history pardons ambition. We celebrate progress, rarely pausing to trace the blood beneath it. Every great reformer, every “visionary,” seems to come with a ledger of sins. Perhaps that’s what power really is: not purity, but proportion. A balance between what you build and what you break.

And when I look at modern politics, I feel the same ache of uncertainty. The names change, the slogans evolve, but the game remains the same. Some rise promising justice, others order — all, in time, reveal their price. Maybe there’s no such thing as a “right side” in politics. Only tides. And you either rise with them, or get swept beneath.

Sometimes I wonder if humanity ever learns, or if we simply keep repainting the same old tragedies in brighter colors. But perhaps that’s the secret of civilization — it isn’t built by saints. It’s built by men who dare, sin, and leave us to argue over which part mattered more: the dream or the damage.
