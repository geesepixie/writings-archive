As I watch the quiet violence of the natural world unfold, creatures chasing, fleeing, breaking, surviving, I find myself standing at a distance that feels both close and impossibly far, wondering not at what happens, but at what is felt. For what reaches me are only signs: a movement, a cry, a body resisting what harms it. And yet I cannot step inside that moment, cannot inhabit the hidden interior where pain either sharpens or softens into something I cannot name.

We say they suffer, and perhaps they do. We have reason to believe so, nerves that mirror ours, reactions that echo what we recognize as distress. It would be careless, even cruel, to deny that. And still, there remains a silence beneath all of it, a distance between what is seen and what is lived, as though reality itself keeps a part of its truth veiled.

Even among ourselves, where understanding should be simplest, it slips. There are those who walk through what we imagine would shatter us, and when they return, they speak of something altered: pain that dulled at the edges, fear that never fully arrived, moments swallowed by adrenaline or lost to memory entirely. From the outside, it looks unbearable. From within, it is… different. Not unreal, not insignificant, but not always what we feared it would be.

And so I wonder, if this distance exists even between one human and another, what of the distance between us and every other living creature? How much of what we imagine is truth, and how much is the echo of our own fears, projected outward onto a world we do not fully understand?

For if God is as we know Him to be, a mercy that exceeds both our understanding and our imagination, then it becomes difficult to believe that what appears before us is all that exists. Not that harm is an illusion, nor that pain is absent, but that its essence, its inner weight, may not rest upon these creatures as heavily as we assume.

Perhaps what we see is real, but incomplete. Perhaps the outward wound does not fully reveal the inward experience. Perhaps there is a gentleness, unseen and unmeasured, woven quietly into moments that look, from afar, unbearably harsh.

This does not absolve us of care. If anything, it deepens it. For we act based on what we can see, and what we can see still calls for compassion, for restraint, for the quiet effort to lessen harm wherever it appears. But beyond our actions lies a boundary we cannot cross, a limit to what we can know.

And at that boundary, I choose not to fill the silence with the worst of my imaginings.

Instead, I leave room for mercy.

Not as denial, but as recognition, that there are dimensions of existence we do not touch, that the soul, whatever it may hold, is not fully exposed to us. That what appears as suffering may be held, softened, or transformed in ways we cannot perceive.

So I remain here, between what is seen and what is believed, carrying both without forcing them into conflict.

For we witness the signs of pain, but not the depth of its experience. And where sight falls short, it is not unreasonable to trust that mercy continues, unseen, where we can no longer follow.
